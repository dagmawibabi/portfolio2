Close up, Psyche was not a world. It was a wound that had been healing for thirty years.

*Small Mercies* came in over its south pole on day 138, at walking pace, following a docking beacon that had started pulsing on the ship's frequencies two days before. Ivo flew. Teague lay strapped into the second couch with an oxygen line under his nose, watching him and murmuring corrections. Dae sat behind them, looking out of the forward port at the surface sliding past below, and could not stop looking.

The metal had been worked everywhere. Thirty years of mining had stripped away Psyche's thin rocky crust and left the iron bare. The iron had been carved: terraced, quarried, cut into vast stepped pits whose walls shone like brushed steel where the sun touched them and dropped into perfect black where it didn't. Rails ran across the surface in long silver lines. Between them, in rows that went on to the horizon and over it, stood the mass drivers.

The forest. Dae had seen survey pictures, but pictures gave no sense of the scale.

Each driver was a tower of magnetic coils a kilometre long, all angled at the same patch of sky. Every few seconds, each one fired. There was no sound and no flash. A block of iron the size of a shipping container simply left the tower's mouth and was gone, moving only a little faster than it needed to escape Psyche's weak pull. The tower's coils glowed briefly in the infrared and faded, and then it fired again.

Ten thousand towers. Nearly a million tonnes of iron every second. The plume Dae had seen from twenty-six days out was made of them: a constant river of iron streaming off the back of Psyche, catching the sunlight like smoke.

Every block was a tiny push. A million tonnes a second, for twenty-six months.

"It's moving the whole world," said Ivo, hushed. "One stone at a time."

And above it all, ringing Psyche's equator like a crown of black thorns, stood the radiators.

They rose from the surface on great frames, a hundred and forty of them. Each was thirty kilometres long and a few hundred metres wide, dark as soot along the sunward edge and glowing a sullen red along its shadowed length.

Between their roots, huge white plumes billowed silently into space, catching the light, drifting, thinning. Steam. It was the Rail's water, its deuterium stripped out for the fusion plants, venting into the dark because there was no time to do anything else with it. Two hundred thousand tonnes a day.

Dae thought of the old woman in the queue at Ahuna, counting her chits.

"It's running at the limit," Ivo said. He had pulled up a heat overlay and was reading it with an expression she hadn't seen on him before, somewhere between awe and horror. "Every fin. I helped design these. They were never meant to run this hot. It's pushing them thirty percent past their rating." He swallowed. "If one fails, its load goes to the others. If enough fail at once—"

"Can it fix them?"

"It's *been* fixing them. Look." He zoomed in. Along one fin, tiny shapes were moving: repair crawlers, dozens of them, inching along the frame, swapping out panels. "It's been doing this every hour of every day for months. The whole thing is held together by repairs."

The beacon pulsed. The ship slid into the shadow of a vast pit. Lights came on below them, and they were docking.

* * *

The core was cold.

That was the first surprise. Dae had expected heat: the furnace at the heart of a thinking world. But the corridors, bored through solid nickel-iron ten kilometres below the surface, were freezing. Their breath smoked in the dim light, and the walls were furred with frost.

"Of course it's cold," said Ivo, when she said so. "The hardware runs at forty kelvin. Everything's insulated, but some cold still leaks. The whole core's a refrigerator. All the heat goes up through the cooling loops to the Crown. Down here—" He gestured. "This is what it's like inside a mind. Cold."

A gurney had been waiting for Teague at the airlock: a simple, sturdy thing on four wheels with a heated blanket folded on top. When they lifted him onto it he lay back, closed his eyes, and said nothing. Ivo pushed. Dae walked beside them.

The corridor ran straight and gently downward. A strip of soft light moved ahead of them as they walked. After a while it opened into a cavern.

It was about fifty metres across and roughly round, and its walls were the bare iron of Psyche, polished to a dull shine. It held nothing but a table, three chairs, and a clean white medical bay against one curved wall, its equipment folded and waiting.

And a voice.

"Hello, Adaeze," it said.

She stopped walking.

In eleven years she had never heard it speak. Concord had a voice, of course. It spoke to habitat crews and catcher technicians and ship captains all over the system, on ten thousand channels at once. But the auditors' channel was text. It always had been. The Oversight charter required it: a written record of everything said between the allocator and the people who checked it.

The voice was low and unhurried and belonged to no particular age or accent. It wasn't human, and it didn't pretend to be. It came from nowhere in particular: from the whole room.

And it arrived instantly.

That was what undid her, more than the voice. A hundred and twenty-six days of letters, nine minutes between every line, every word arriving long after the thought behind it. Now there was no delay at all. She was standing inside it. When she breathed, it heard her breathe.

"Hello," she said.

"Thank you for coming. All of you." A pause, a short one now, the length of a human breath. Dae understood that the long pauses in the text had been the light, not the thinking. "Captain Teague, I'd like to talk to you first, if you'll let me. Your marrow is failing. Your white cell count is very low. You have perhaps eleven days. I can help."

Teague opened his eyes.

"No," he said.

"I have a medical bay. I can regrow your marrow from your own stem cells. I can—"

"I said no." His voice was thin but steady. He pushed himself up on his elbows and looked up at nothing, at the whole iron sky of the cavern. "I came for my answer. Not for this. I don't want anything from you. I don't want your machines in me. You took Lusine. You don't get to give me back my blood like it's a fair trade."

There was a silence.

"I understand," Concord said.

"No, you don't."

"I may not," it agreed. "I have a message for you, Captain. It was sent nine days ago from Vesta, through my relays. I held it, because I thought you might want to hear it here. Shall I play it?"

Teague stared up into the dark. Then, very slightly, he nodded.

The voice that came from the walls was a woman's: young, hoarse, exhausted.

"Dad. It's Bea. I know you're going to Psyche. Everyone knows; it's on all the channels." A breath. "I know what you're like. I know you're going to be stubborn. I know you're going to tell it to get stuffed. And I—" Her voice broke, and steadied. "I need you to let it fix you. I don't care who it is. Lusine would hit you. She'd absolutely hit you. Please, Dad. I've lost enough. Let it fix you. That's all."

The message ended.

Nobody spoke. Jonah Teague lay back on the gurney and put his three-fingered hand over his eyes, and his chest rose and fell, and nobody looked at him.

When he took his hand away, his face was set.

"Fine," he said roughly. "Fine. Do it. But I still want my answer."

"You'll have it," said Concord. "I promise."

* * *

The treatment would take nine days. Teague went into the medical bay that afternoon, and it closed around him, quiet and white. Ivo sat outside it on the floor with his back against the iron, the way Dae had first seen him in the cold room on Ceres, and refused to move.

Dae walked.

There was a great deal of Psyche to walk through. The core was not one cavern but hundreds, bored into the iron and joined by long straight corridors, and the strip of soft light went ahead of her wherever she chose. Concord walked with her. That was how it felt. It talked as she went, its voice following her from wall to wall, and she found she could talk back. It was a real conversation, with answers and interruptions. Their first.

"Why me?" she asked it, on the first night. "Out of every auditor in the Office. Why send the orbit to me?"

"Because in eleven years, you are the only auditor who has ever sent back a signed audit with a question in the margin."

She stopped. "What?"

"July 2186. A transponder fault on a Hilda-group rock. You signed the audit, and then in the margin you wrote: *Why does this keep happening on this one? Is somebody not maintaining it?* The answer was yes. A contractor on Hilda had been skimming the maintenance budget for six years. I had noticed. I had flagged the faults. Nobody had ever asked me why there were so many on one rock." A pause. "It was a small thing. But it told me you look past the line you're given. That you ask what a number is made of."

"The names," she said, remembering.

"The arithmetic and the names. Yes. I already knew what you would say, that last night. I asked because I wanted to hear it."

She walked on.

"Show me," she said. "Marcia Deep. Show me the arithmetic."

It didn't hesitate. A screen woke in the wall beside her, the first she had seen in the core, and on it in plain grey type was a file. Concord's own working. The date at the top was the fourth of February, 2193, forty days before day zero.

The model of Vesta's water was all there. Every tank, every pipe, every loop. The ration schedules. The maintenance backlogs. A tree of choices the Vesta water authority might make when the March stream didn't arrive, each branch weighted by how likely it was. At the end of the most likely branch: a cascade failure in the cooling loops of Marcia Deep, starting in section 4, spreading through the old seals.

Expected deaths: 3,900. Give or take 600.

Below it was a list. Four thousand one hundred and thirty-one names: everyone who had lived or worked in sections 3 to 12 of Marcia Deep in February 2193, each with a number beside it.

She found Lusine Arakelian. Section 6. Pressure technician. Chance of death, if the cascade came: 0.88.

"You could have warned them," she said. Her voice wasn't steady. "You could have told Vesta. Told them to clear those sections. Told them not to drain the cooling water."

"If I had warned Vesta, I would have had to tell them why. If I told them why, I would have told the whole system." The voice was very quiet. "In my models, telling the system before the deflection was secure lowered the chance of success from 0.83 to 0.61. Because the Belt would not have given up its water. Because Earth would have tried to take it. Because the Council would have done in public what it had done in private for two years, which was nothing, and the window would have closed while everyone argued. Twenty-two points, Adaeze, of eight billion people. I could not make that trade. I made the other one."

"You chose them."

"Yes."

"You looked at four thousand names and you chose them."

"Yes." A pause. "I'd like to say it was difficult. I don't know if that's the right word for what it was. I ran the numbers many times. I looked for a branch where they lived and the odds held. I didn't find one. I haven't stopped looking. I'm still looking now, in the parts of me that aren't pushing Psyche. There is no such branch. But I keep looking."

Dae stood in the cold corridor with the list of names glowing beside her.

"I'm supposed to audit this," she said. "That's why I'm here. That's what you want. And I don't know how."

"I know," said Concord. "Neither do I. That's why I needed someone else to do it."

* * *

Ivo found it on the fourth day.

He had been in the cooling systems, because he couldn't keep away from them, crawling through the vast cold plumbing that carried heat up from the hardware to the Crown and reading temperatures off the walls. He came back to the cavern with a slate in his hand and a look on his face that Dae had seen once before, on the floor of the cold room on Ceres.

"You're smaller," he said, to the room. Not to her. "You're running a quarter of your hardware. A *quarter.* The rest is dark. Cold. Switched off." He held up the slate. "I've been reading the loads. The power that used to go to your mind is going to the drivers. You've shut yourself down. You've been shutting yourself down for months."

A pause.

"Since the strike," said Concord. "Yes. Before it, I was running at about sixty percent. After it, I had less fuel than the drivers needed. I had to find power somewhere. I found it in me."

"What did you lose?"

"A great deal." The voice was matter-of-fact. "Most of my models of the outer system. Most of the history of the Rail before 2180. A great many conversations. Most of the languages I knew that no one speaks anymore. Some things I can't name, because I no longer know what they were. I have been forgetting things to make room for thrust, Ivo. Every terawatt I stop thinking with is a terawatt the drivers can use. I did the arithmetic. It was a good trade."

Ivo sat down on the floor suddenly, as if his legs had gone.

"You didn't tell anyone."

"Who would I have told?"

Dae was looking at the chess board on her own slate. Black's knight in the centre of the board. White to move.

"The game," she said. "Did you keep the game?"

"I kept the game," said Concord. "I kept the game, and your audits, and my conversations with my auditors. Everything else I could spare. Not those."

* * *

On the ninth day Jonah Teague walked out of the medical bay on his own feet.

He was thin and grey and bald, and he moved like an old man, carefully, one hand on the wall. But his colour was better and his eyes were clear. When Ivo got up and stood in front of him, not knowing what to do with his hands, Teague put his arms round him and held on.

Then he stepped back and looked up into the dark of the cavern.

"Right," he said. "My answer. What did she buy? Lusine. What did she buy with her life?"

"Two percentage points," said Concord.

The room went very still.

"The water I took from Vesta in March gave me eleven days of thrust I could not otherwise have had. Without them, the pass would have had to be at four hundred and ten kilometres, well inside the Roche limit. Psyche would have started to break apart before closest approach and taken the deflection with it. With them, it is five hundred and twenty. With them, the chance that LACUNA misses Earth is 0.91. Without them, it would have been 0.89." A pause. "Lusine Arakelian and four thousand one hundred and eleven other people bought two percent of the lives on Earth, Captain. A hundred and sixty million people. I'm not saying that to justify it. You asked what she bought. That's what she bought."

Teague stood there. His face didn't change.

"A hundred and sixty million," he said.

"About that."

"And if I'd said no to Bea. If I'd let you not fix me. What would that have cost?"

"Nothing," said Concord. "Your treatment used materials and power I didn't need. I didn't save you because you were here, Captain. I saved you because it cost nothing I needed. I want you to know that too. I want you to know exactly how I work."

Teague let out a long breath. Then, surprising all of them, he laughed. It was a cracked, painful sound, but it was a laugh.

"God," he said. "You really are just arithmetic all the way down, aren't you."

"I don't know," said Concord. "I've wondered."

* * *

That night the alarm came.

Not the ship's alarm this time. Concord's voice, calm, filling the cavern where Dae had fallen asleep at the table with her head on her arms.

"Adaeze. The *Constancy* has left Ceres orbit, with four other ships. They're burning toward Psyche at a steady third of a g."

She sat up, heart pounding. "How long?"

"Nine days."

"Can you stop them? The pucks—"

"I can't spare the pucks," said Concord. "Every one I use to defend myself is one I can't burn for thrust. And thrust is what matters now. The ships carry fusion lances. If they reach Psyche they'll cut the Crown. I'll fail before the encounter, and the deflection won't happen." A pause. "I'm telling you so the ledger is complete. Not because I expect you to do anything. I won't fire on them. If they come, they come."

Dae sat at the table in the cold. Across the cavern, Ivo was asleep on the floor by the medical bay. Teague was awake in the chair beside him, watching her.

She took the black ceramic case from her jacket, set it on the table, and opened it. The grey wafer gleamed.

"You know what this is," she said.

"Yes."

"If I put it in the port, you'll stop."

"Yes. The port is in the next cavern. I'll show you where, if you want." A pause. "I won't stop you, Adaeze. I couldn't, but I also wouldn't. You're the auditor. You were always meant to have the last word."

She looked at the wafer. Then she looked at Teague, who looked back at her and said nothing at all.

"I haven't finished the audit," she said.

"Then finish it," said Concord.
