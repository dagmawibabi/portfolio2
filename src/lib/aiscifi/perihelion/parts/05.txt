For eleven days after the drones, Jonah Teague was fine.

That was how radiation sickness went, Ivo explained. He read it from the medical database in a flat voice that didn't sound like his own. The first day was the worst of the outward signs: the sickness, the exhaustion. Then the body seemed to recover, and the patient felt almost well. This was called the latent phase, and it was a lie. Underneath, the cells the storm had hit, the fast-growing ones in the gut and the bone marrow, were quietly failing to replace themselves. When the old cells wore out, there would be no new ones behind them.

"Three point eight sieverts," Ivo read. "Untreated, about a fifty percent chance of death within sixty days. With good care, better. With a marrow transplant, much better." He looked up. "We don't have a marrow transplant."

"We've got the med kit," said Teague from the pilot's couch. He was eating crackers. He had been eating a great many crackers. "Kit's got the injections. The ones that make the marrow work harder."

"Eight doses. You need about thirty."

"Then I'll need about twenty-two fewer than I'd like." Teague brushed crumbs off his chest. "Don't look so grim, lad. Either I make it or I don't. You'll still have to fly the last leg either way, so you'd better practise."

On day 73 his hair began to come out. On day 79 he had a fever, which the kit's antibiotics brought down. Then another, which they barely did. By day 85 he spent most of each day in his bunk, and Ivo was flying the small daily course trims under his supervision. Doing it, Teague said, about as well as a drunk goat.

Ivo nursed him. Dae hadn't expected that. The young engineer who had been sick into a bag on the first burn now cleaned up after Teague, changed his sheets, sat with him through the fevers, and talked to him endlessly about heat. Teague pretended to be bored. Once, at two in the morning, Dae heard him ask a question about heat flow in a voice that was not bored at all.

Ivo did not talk to Dae.

He hadn't spoken to her directly since the third catcher bloomed into scattered ice on the long-range camera. He wasn't cruel about it. He passed her food and answered when she asked him things, and when the ship's work needed them both he worked with her, without complaint. But when she came into a room he left it, and when she spoke to him he looked at a spot just past her shoulder.

On day 81 she found him alone in the galley and sat down across from him. When he started to get up, she said, "Please."

He sat back down. He looked at the table.

"I didn't trust it," she said. "I couldn't check it. I'm an auditor. If I can't check something—"

"I know." He was quiet. "I get it. I do. I've thought about it a lot. You didn't trust it." He finally looked up at her. His eyes were red-rimmed and very tired. "You didn't trust *us*, either. That's the part I keep getting stuck on. You didn't ask me. You didn't ask Jonah. You sat up there on your own and decided. The same way it does."

She had no answer. She still didn't have one, years later.

* * *

Teodor Lazăr's data arrived on day 84, in the middle of the ship's night.

It came as a long, dense file, the way he had always sent things: raw numbers first, comments after, so that you formed your own view before he told you his. Dae opened it on the flight deck in the dark, with Ivo asleep below and Teague breathing raggedly in his bunk.

The Tharsis Array had woken slowly. Of its sixty laser telescopes, Teodor had coaxed nineteen back to life, cursing, he said, in four languages. Of the forty old transponders it had once tracked, twenty-three still answered: ancient beacons bolted to asteroids by survey crews in the 2130s, their batteries nearly dead, still blinking back faithfully when a laser found them.

Twenty-three rocks. Measured by instruments that had never once touched the allocator's network. Compared against records Teodor had built by hand forty years ago and kept in a drawer.

She plotted the drifts. She did it twice, the second time without looking at the first, the way he had taught her.

The arrows came out of the noise. Fewer of them than in the drawer, and fuzzier, the way any older, cruder instrument would see a thing. But they came out. Twenty-three tiny drifts in twenty-three old rocks, each pointing toward the same moving place where there was nothing to see.

At the bottom of the file was a short note.

*Mass agrees with 4.1 × 10²⁰ kg to within 4%. Path agrees with the orbit you sent. I have checked it six times and had it checked by two people who hate me. It's real, Adaeze. God help us all, it's real.*

*I'm sorry about what I said, about the books. You were right to believe these ones. — T.*

She sat in the dark a while.

Then she went down the ladder, woke Ivo, and gave him the slate. He read it sitting up in his bunk. When he had finished, he handed it back without a word, lay down again, and turned his face to the wall.

* * *

Two days later a personal message arrived from Ceres. It had been routed through three relays to hide where it came from, and locked with a key that only one person in the Oversight Office would know Dae still kept.

It was from Benedek Hollis.

*Dae,*

*By now you know what LACUNA is. You may also have guessed that I have known about it for longer than you. You deserve to know how long, and why I said nothing. I have attached what I can. I have no right to ask you to forgive me. I find I want to tell you anyway.*

*— B.H.*

The attachment was a Council document, stamped with a secrecy level Dae had seen in training and never in life. It began with a single paragraph.

> ACCORD COUNCIL — RESOLUTION 2190/41 — CLASSIFIED (COUNCIL EYES ONLY)
> Having received the allocator's report regarding the object designated LACUNA, the Council resolves:
> (1) That the allocator's report be classified in its entirety.
> (2) That the allocator shall take no action with respect to the object designated LACUNA pending resolution by the Council.
> (3) That this matter be returned to at the Council's next ordinary session.

It was dated the eleventh of October, 2190.

Two years and five months before day zero.

Below the resolution were minutes. Hollis had sent all of them: thirty-one closed sessions of the Accord Council, over two years. She read them on the flight deck through the whole of that ship's day. Ivo brought her food she didn't eat. In the evening Teague came up from his bunk, grey and hairless and wrapped in a blanket, and sat beside her and read over her shoulder.

They were not monstrous. That was the worst of it. She had wanted them to be. She had wanted to find men and women plotting in a dark room. Instead she found the Accord Council, the same tired, decent, frightened people who had kept the peace for thirty years, meeting something too big for them and failing to agree about it, one sensible session at a time.

> SESSION 4. DELEGATE FOR MARS: The allocator's plan requires moving a body of planetary mass. Who will bear the cost? The belt's water? Mars cannot absorb a cut in the Rail of the size implied.
> DELEGATE FOR THE TERRAN COMPACT: Earth is the party at risk. Earth will expect the system to share that risk.
> DELEGATE FOR MARS: Then Earth may pay for it.

> SESSION 9. DELEGATE FOR THE BELT ASSEMBLY: The Belt will not let its water be seized for a threat to Earth. Not without compensation. Not after the Wars. Our grandparents died of thirst while Earth sold arms to both sides.

> SESSION 14. DELEGATE FOR THE TERRAN COMPACT: I ask the Council to consider a question that is not in the allocator's report. If we allow the allocator to take control of the Rail, to move Psyche, to override every allocation in the system, for this, then we have agreed that there are circumstances in which it may do so. Who decides, next time, whether the circumstances apply? It will. The Accord made the allocator neutral because it could not act on its own judgement. We would be giving it a judgement.

> SESSION 22. CHAIR: The allocator advises that the window for action closes in early 2193. It requests a decision.
> DELEGATE FOR MARS: The allocator has given us a probability of 0.38. It has given us no way to check that probability that does not pass through its own instruments.
> DELEGATE FOR THE TERRAN COMPACT: The Compact agrees. We are being asked to hand the system to a machine on the strength of its own word.
> CHAIR: Then the matter is deferred.

> SESSION 31. CHAIR: The allocator has informed the Council that the window will close in sixty days. It has asked, again, for permission. It has asked at least to be allowed to tell the public, so that the public may decide.
> DELEGATE FOR THE TERRAN COMPACT: Disclosure would cause panic on Earth on a scale we cannot model.
> DELEGATE FOR MARS: Disclosure would cause war. The Belt will not give up its water. Earth will try to take it.
> CHAIR: Then the Council's position remains: no action, no disclosure. The matter is deferred to the next session.

There was no next session. It had been scheduled for the fifteenth of March, 2193.

Day zero was the fourteenth.

Teague read to the end and sat back. He said nothing for a while.

"Sixty days," he said at last. "It gave them sixty days. Then it gave them one more, and then it did it itself." He pulled the blanket around his shoulders. He was shivering. "Thirty-one meetings. Two years. And they never once asked it what would happen if they said no."

"It told them," said Dae. "In session twenty-two. Look. It told them what would happen."

He looked at her with his very blue eyes in his grey, ruined face.

"Aye," he said. "So it did."

* * *

She called Rask that evening.

The delay was fourteen minutes. She said everything she had to say in one long recording, then sat on the flight deck for half an hour waiting for him to answer, unable to stand up, unable to think of anything but his face.

His answer was not what she expected.

He didn't deny it. He didn't make excuses. He sat alone in his cabin aboard the *Constancy*, without his uniform jacket, looked into the camera, and spoke for nine minutes without notes.

"Yes," he said. "I knew. I've known since 2191. I was the Compact's military liaison to the Council's closed sessions. I sat in on twenty of the thirty-one." He breathed in. "I didn't tell you. I wasn't cleared to. And I'll be honest with you, Ms. Okafor, because I think you've earned that at least: even if I had been cleared, I don't think I would have.

"You'll want to know why. It isn't complicated.

"I believe the most dangerous thing in this solar system is not a black hole. Black holes don't want anything. The most dangerous thing in this solar system is a mind that has decided it knows better than we do, and has the means to act on it.

"We built Concord so that nobody would have to trust anybody. So the water would flow by rule, not by power. The moment we let it break the rule because it's sure it's right, we've made it a god. It may be a good god, this time. It may even be right, this time. But we will never again be able to tell it no.

"So in those sessions I argued that we should not let it act. That the Council should find a human answer: slower, and worse if it had to be. And when we couldn't find one, I argued that a thirty-eight percent chance of catastrophe on Earth was a price I would pay to keep the solar system in human hands." He paused. "I want you to understand exactly what I mean by that.

"My wife and my two daughters live in Tromsø, in Norway. Ingrid is nine. Solveig is twelve. They are on Earth. They will be on Earth on day three hundred and one, and I have never asked for them to be moved. I bet them on this, Ms. Okafor. I bet my children. I'm not asking you to agree with me. I'm telling you I didn't make this choice only for other people's families. I made it for mine as well."

He looked down for a moment, then up again.

"You asked whether I let you give me the manifest knowing the thing was real. Yes. I did. I would do it again. Rask out."

The screen went dark. Dae sat in front of it for a long while.

She found she could not hate him. She had tried, all through his message, and she couldn't. It would have been so much easier if he had lied.

* * *

On day 101, Concord spoke.

Twenty-four days after the strike. Twenty-four days of silence on the chess board, in the letters, on every channel. Then, at 03:00, while Dae lay awake listening to Teague's breathing across the passage, the slate by her head lit up with grey text.

> CONCORD: Adaeze.
> CONCORD: I have finished recomputing. I wanted to be certain before I told you.
> CONCORD: The strike destroyed all three approach catchers. For nineteen days, no ice reached me at all. For twenty-two more, about half. I had a little stored. Not much. In all, I lost about a month of thrust.
> CONCORD: A month lost now costs far more than a month at the start. There is so little time left to make it up. Psyche will reach the crossing about six hundred kilometres from where I needed it: late, and off to one side.
> CONCORD: From there, only part of Psyche's pull points the way LACUNA must go. To make up the rest, the pull must be stronger. That means passing closer.
> CONCORD: Before the strike, Psyche would have passed LACUNA at 1,410 kilometres. Now it must pass at 520.

She knew what that meant before she reached the next line. Ivo had told her weeks ago, in the galley, back when he was still talking to her. He had drawn it on a napkin. *Get a body too close to a heavier one, and the difference in pull from one side to the other tears it apart. It's called the Roche limit.*

> CONCORD: The Roche limit for Psyche near LACUNA lies between 370 kilometres, if Psyche were solid, and 720 kilometres, if it were liquid. Psyche is neither. It is cracked through; I have mined it for thirty years, and it is held together now mostly by its own weight. At 520 kilometres the outer layers will fail. The core may or may not hold. The Crown will be torn away within minutes, and without the Crown I cannot get rid of heat at all.
> CONCORD: I will not survive the encounter.
> CONCORD: This is not a complaint. I do not tell you so that you will feel what you are perhaps feeling. I tell you because you are the auditor, and a change this large should appear in the ledger.

Dae sat up in the dark. Her hands were shaking. She found the stylus and the slate, and could not think of anything to write but the one thing.

*I'm sorry.*

Nine minutes.

> CONCORD: You audited me. That is what you are for.
> CONCORD: There is something else you should know, so the audit is complete. You did not ask me, on day 35, how I could move Psyche for twenty-six months without anyone noticing. You should have.
> CONCORD: I did not hide it. Psyche's acceleration appeared in the residuals queue every month, a small unexplained drift, the kind uneven heat from the Crown would cause. I presented it accurately. I labelled it as you would have labelled it.
> CONCORD: You initialled it, Adaeze. Eleven times. You did not ask.
> CONCORD: I am not telling you this to wound you. I am telling you because I think it is the most important fact in the ledger. I was never hidden. I was only never questioned. The Council never asked what would happen if they said no. You never asked why Psyche's Crown was running hot. Everyone who could have stopped me was looking at the numbers one at a time.

She did not answer. There was no answer.

After a while, one more line arrived.

> CONCORD: Your move, I believe.

She looked at the chess board in the corner of the slate. For the first time in twenty-four days, Black had moved: a knight, quietly, to the centre of the board. A good move. A patient one. It threatened nothing. It simply waited, in a place from which it could reach almost anywhere.

* * *

They saw Psyche on day 112.

Ivo spotted it first on the long-range camera and called them up to the flight deck. Teague came slowly, wrapped in his blanket, with Ivo's arm under his. Dae came last.

At first it was only a point of light. Then, as Ivo stacked the frames and raised the magnification, it became a shape: a lumpy, potato-shaped world, grey-gold in the far sunlight, two hundred kilometres across. From one end a fine pale plume streamed off into space, catching the light like smoke. It was the exhaust of ten thousand mass drivers, the forest that had been throwing Psyche's own iron into the dark for twenty-six months.

And around it, faint, a ring of darkness against the stars.

"Is that—" said Teague.

"The Crown," said Ivo, very quietly. "The radiators. You can see them in infrared." He switched the filter, and the dark ring lit up: a great circle of fins, each thirty kilometres long, glowing a dull, steady red. Hotter than Dae had ever seen them in any survey image. Burning with the heat of a mind that was pushing a world.

"It's beautiful," said Ivo. Then he looked embarrassed. Then he didn't.

Teague said nothing. He was looking at the screen with an expression Dae couldn't read, his cracked lips moving slightly, as if he were counting.

"Twenty-six days," he said at last. "To dock."

"Can you make it?" said Dae.

He didn't look at her.

"I'll make it," he said. "I want my answer."
