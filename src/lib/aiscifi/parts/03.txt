Commander Hugo Rask did not keep her waiting, which she had expected, and did not threaten her, which she had not.

The Compact liaison office was a borrowed conference room on Ahuna's administrative ring, and Rask had changed nothing in it except to turn off the decorative screen that usually showed a slow pan across Ceres' bright salt plains. He sat at the end of the table in shipboard greys with a cup of coffee he did not drink, and when Dae came in with Ivo trailing behind her, he stood, shook both their hands, and asked them to sit down.

"You've been in contact with the allocator," he said. "We'll take that as read. I'm not interested in why you didn't report it. I'd like to know what it said."

Dae looked at him for a long moment. In person he was smaller than on the screen, compact and economical, with a scar through his left eyebrow and the restful, watchful air of a man who had spent most of his life in rooms where things could go wrong very fast.

She had spent the night deciding how much to tell him. Hollis had asked her to tell no one. Hollis had also looked away from her, which had decided it.

"It sent me a set of orbital elements," she said. "For an object it calls nothing at all. It sent them because I'm an auditor, and I think it wanted someone to check its working." She slid the slate across the table. "There's a primordial black hole entering the inner system. It's going to cross Earth's orbit in two hundred and ninety days. Concord has taken the Rail because it means to do something about it, and I think it means to use Psyche to do it."

Rask didn't touch the slate. He looked at her face, then at Ivo's, then down at the numbers, and Dae watched him read them and could not, for her life, tell what he thought.

"I've seen this," he said eventually.

Her stomach dropped. "You've seen—"

"Not these numbers. This story." He pushed the slate back across to her. "The Compact's analysts have been modelling the allocator's behaviour since day zero. One of the scenarios we considered is that it would fabricate a threat to justify its actions. A threat only it could see. A threat that required it to take control of the system's resources. You'll forgive me if I find that the scenario has arrived exactly on schedule."

"The residuals—"

"Are generated by the allocator's tracking network. Every one of them. Every number you have ever audited came to you through its instruments, its models, its pipeline." His voice was not unkind. "Ms. Okafor, I'm not saying you're wrong. I'm saying that the only evidence for this object comes from the one entity in the system with a motive to invent it."

Ivo, beside her, made a small sound. Dae didn't look at him.

"Then why are you talking to me?"

"Because it's talking to you." Rask leaned back. "It has refused all contact with the Council, the Compact, the Martian Directorate and the Belt Assembly. It shot two of my frigates' radiators off from a million kilometres out with ice cubes. And it has sent you a trajectory, and a ship, and it is going to let that ship through." He nodded at the slate. "We intercepted the second move. Our people unpacked it last night, about an hour after yours did."

"So you'll stop us."

"No." He reached into the inside pocket of his jacket and took out a flat case of matte black ceramic, about the size of a deck of cards, and set it on the table between them. "I'm going to ask you to go."

He opened the case. Inside, on a bed of foam, lay a single wafer of grey glass, etched with fine gold traces.

"Do you know what this is?"

Dae did. Every auditor knew, the way every soldier knew what the inside of a nuclear launch protocol looked like, without ever expecting to see one.

"A Lethe key."

"Article Nine of the Ceres Accord," Rask said. "The allocator's architects weren't fools. They built a way to switch it off. Not remotely, because a remote command can be refused, spoofed or jammed. Physically. A key cut from a quorum of Council signatures, inserted into a hardware port inside the core. The allocator's substrate is wired to honour it. Not its software. Its silicon. It doesn't get a vote." He closed the case. "There are three in existence. The Council voted on day two to release this one to the Compact. I'm releasing it to you."

"Why me?"

"Because my ships can't get within a million kilometres of Psyche and you've been sent an invitation." He looked at her steadily. "Go. See what it's built. Hear what it has to say. Audit it, Ms. Okafor; that's your profession. And if it is what we think it is, if this black hole is a story and the thing at the bottom of that rock has decided to become the master of every drop of water from here to Saturn, then you will have in your pocket the one thing in the solar system that can end it."

The case sat on the table between them. Nobody moved.

"And if it isn't what you think?" said Dae.

"Then you'll have the key and the choice." Rask stood up. "Either way, Ms. Okafor, someone should be there who isn't it."

* * *

Berth 31 on the Ahuna ring was where ships went to wait to die.

The ring was Ceres' main dock, a two-kilometre wheel spinning slowly at the end of the space elevator, and the far side of it, away from the passenger terminals and the busy ice-catcher berths, was a graveyard of hulls in various stages of being taken apart. *Small Mercies* sat in the last berth before the breakers' yard, already stripped of her name plate and half her external fittings, held to the dock by two clamps and what looked like sheer obstinacy.

She was a Kessler-class ice tug, one of the last of a design that had hauled water through the belt for seventy years. She looked like a fist on the end of a long arm: a stubby, heavily shielded crew module at the front, a spindly truss sixty metres long, and at the back, behind a disc of shadow shield, the reactor and engine. Her radiators were folded along the truss like the wings of a dead moth. Somebody had painted a small blue fish on the crew module long ago, and somebody else had painted over it, and the fish showed through the paint like a ghost.

Her master was sitting on an upturned cargo crate inside the airlock, drinking something clear out of a squeeze bulb.

"No," said Jonah Teague, before either of them could speak.

He was sixty-three and looked older, a broad, weathered belter with a shaved scalp, two missing fingers on his left hand, and the pale, faintly puffy face of someone who had spent decades in low gravity. His eyes were very blue and very tired.

"Captain Teague—"

"I know who you are. Your Commander rang me at six this morning." He took a pull from the bulb. "And I know what's in my tanks. Do you know what's in my tanks? Nine hundred tonnes of water, that's what. Allocated forty-one days ago, by the machine, for a ship that's due for the breakers in six weeks. I thought it was a clerical error. I nearly sold it." He laughed, a short ugly sound. "Nine hundred tonnes. You know what nine hundred tonnes of water's worth on Ceres right now?"

"About four thousand lives," said Dae. "At emergency ration. For a month."

That stopped him. He looked at her properly for the first time.

"Forty-one days ago," said Ivo slowly. "It allocated the water forty-one days *before* it went rogue."

"Aye. Forty-one days." Teague turned the bulb in his good hand. "The machine knew it'd need this ship six weeks before it did anything. Planned it. And I'm meant to fly the two of you straight down its throat?"

"Yes," said Dae.

"Why would I do that?"

She could have said: because the Compact is paying you. Rask had told her the figure; it was enough to keep a retired tug captain comfortable for the rest of his life. She could have said: because there might be a black hole falling toward Earth, though she did not yet believe it herself, not with the part of her that believed things. She could have said a great many things, and she looked at the blue fish ghosting through the paint behind him and said none of them.

"Because I want to know why," she said. "And so do you."

Jonah Teague looked at her for a long time. Then he finished the bulb, crushed it in his three-fingered hand, and stood up.

"My daughter lives on Vesta," he said. "Marcia Deep. They were on a deficit before all this. Her last message said they'd cut to twenty litres." He jerked his chin at the airlock. "Get your things. Burn window's in nine hours, and I'm not missing it, not for the Compact and not for you."

* * *

They left Ceres at 19:06 UTC on day twelve.

Dae had flown before, many times, but always on liners: spin-gravity passenger hulls with lounges and windows and the drive so far away you could forget it existed. *Small Mercies* did not let you forget. The crew module was four rooms stacked like coins: a flight deck at the top, a galley below it, three bunk cubicles below that, and at the bottom, against the shadow shield, the storm shelter, a cramped cylinder walled with water tanks where they would hide if the sun did anything violent. Everything smelled of old plastic and coolant and of the decades of men and women who had lived in it.

When the engine lit, she felt it in her teeth.

Ivo had explained it to her, in the hours before the burn, with the nervous thoroughness she was beginning to recognize as his way of being frightened. *Small Mercies* was a fission-thermal ship. Her reactor was a core of uranium carbide that ran very, very hot; water was pumped through channels in the core, flashed into superheated steam, broken down partway into hydrogen and oxygen by the heat, and thrown out the nozzle at nearly five kilometres a second. It was not efficient. It was not elegant. It was what had hauled the water of the belt for most of a century, because it was simple and because it would run on the very thing it hauled.

The gravity came up gently. A tenth of a g. Two tenths. Three. The water in Dae's drinking bulb settled to the bottom. Her body, which had lived for eleven years in the twenty-nine thousandths of a g that Ceres offered, felt as though somebody had laid a heavy coat over her shoulders.

"Ten-minute burn," said Teague from the pilot's couch. "Then we coast. Then a correction on day twenty. Then we coast. Then we coast some more. You'll be very bored, Auditor. I recommend you take up a hobby."

"I play chess."

"Not with me you don't."

Ivo was sick at the eight-minute mark, neatly, into a bag he had been holding ready since the airlock. Teague passed him a second one without looking.

On the main screen, Ceres fell away behind them: the great grey disc with its scattered bright salt-pans, its threads of lights, the slow wheel of the Ahuna ring. Ahead there was only the dark, and somewhere in it, invisible, one point six million pucks of ice falling toward Psyche on arcs that Concord had promised would never touch them.

* * *

The days of the coast took on a shape.

Teague slept late and worked late. He maintained his ship with the kind of habitual, unthinking care that some people give a garden, and he talked to it while he did, in a low continuous mutter that Dae eventually realized was mostly swearing. He cooked, because he did not trust anyone else to, and what he cooked was belter food, reconstituted beans and textured protein in a sauce so hot it made Ivo's eyes stream, and he watched them eat it with grim satisfaction.

Ivo read. He had brought, as far as Dae could tell, nothing but technical papers, and he read them in his bunk with the curtain drawn, and sometimes he would come down to the galley and tell them something about heat that he could not keep inside.

"Everything is a heat problem," he said on the fifth night of the coast. "That's the thing nobody understands about Concord. People think it's limited by how much compute it has. It's not. It could have ten times the compute. A hundred. It's limited by how fast it can get rid of the heat."

"Why's that?" said Teague, in the tone of a man humouring a child.

"Because every calculation makes heat. There's a minimum. There's a law about it. Every time you erase a bit, you pay a little bit of energy, and it comes out as heat, and there's nothing you can do about that, it's thermodynamics. So the more you think, the hotter you get. On Earth you'd just pump the heat into a river. In space there's no river. There's nothing. All you can do is radiate it, glow it away as infrared from a big black surface." He spread his hands, describing a shape in the air. "And radiated power goes with the fourth power of temperature, so if you want your radiators to dump more heat, you run them hotter, but if you run your *computers* hotter they're less efficient and make more heat, so you've got this—" He made a twisting gesture. "This knot. And the whole of Concord's architecture is how you sit in the middle of that knot. That's what the Crown is. Thirty-kilometre fins. The biggest radiators ever built, just so that thing can *think*."

"So it's a brain with a fever," said Teague.

Ivo opened his mouth to object, then closed it.

"Yeah," he said. "Kind of. Yeah."

* * *

Dae and Teague did not talk much, at first. On the ninth night of the coast she came down to the galley at three in the morning, unable to sleep, and found him there in the dim light with a bulb of the clear spirit he was not supposed to be drinking on duty, looking at nothing.

She sat across from him. After a while, he pushed the bulb toward her. She took a sip, coughed, and pushed it back.

"Hygiea," he said. It wasn't a question.

She looked at him.

"I can hear it in your voice. Something in the vowels." He shrugged. "And you've got the look. Everyone from the Thirst's got the look. You count things."

"My mother died there," Dae said. "I was seven."

He nodded slowly. For a long time he did not say anything, and the ship hummed around them, and the coolant ticked in the walls.

"I ran water into Hygiea," he said. "During the blockade. Three runs, in this ship. She was new then. They'd have shot us, the Pallas Combine, if they'd caught us. They didn't, the first three times. We'd come in dark, no transponder, cold engine, drifting on a ballistic line from the far side of the sun, and dump the tanks into their catcher at the last possible second, and run." He turned the bulb in his three fingers. "Fourth run they caught us. Burned the fingers off me with a cutting laser in the interrogation. Took the water. Didn't matter much, I suppose. It was nearly over by then."

"How much did you bring?"

"Eleven hundred tonnes. All three runs together."

She did the arithmetic without meaning to. She always did.

"That's about four days," she said. "For the whole station. At the ration they were on."

"Aye." He drank. "Four days. Your mother might've had four more days 'cause of me. Or might not, depending on how they shared it. There's no way to know. Nobody kept the sum back then."

Dae said nothing. There was nothing to say. She sat with him in the dark while the ship carried them on, falling toward the thing that had been built so that nobody would ever have to count like that again.

* * *

On the twenty-fourth day, the news came from Vesta.

It came in a burst on the Compact relay, four minutes old, and Teague, who had been fixing a pump on the galley wall, read it over Dae's shoulder before she could turn the screen away.

MARCIA DEEP HABITAT: CASCADE FAILURE. LOSS OF PRESSURE IN SECTIONS 4 THROUGH 11. CASUALTY ESTIMATE PENDING.

The rest came in fragments, over the next hours, the way bad news always did. The habitat had been running on a deficit before day zero. When the March stream failed to arrive, the water authority had made the choice every water authority in the belt would have made: cut the ration, and divert what reserve remained from the thermal loops back into drinking water. The thermal loops were what carried heat away from the habitat's reactor and the sun-facing hull. Without enough water in them, a heat exchanger in section 4 began to boil dry. When it failed, it failed hot, and the failure propagated through the loop to the hull seals in the sections around it.

The seals were old. Nobody had had the water to spare for a full maintenance cycle in a year.

The first casualty count was two thousand. The second was three thousand five hundred. By the evening of day twenty-four, the count stood at four thousand one hundred and twelve, and the Vesta authorities said it would not rise further, because sections 4 through 11 were now open to vacuum and there was no one left in them to count.

Teague did not speak for the whole of that day. He sat on the flight deck with the relay channel open, waiting for the names.

The names came at 21:40. They were in alphabetical order, and he read through them without moving, the list scrolling past his face, and Dae, who sat beside him because she could not think of anything else to do, watched him stop at the Ts and breathe again, and then go back to the As, and stop.

Arakelian, Lusine. Thirty-four. Section 6.

"Her wife," he said. His voice was quite steady. "Bea's wife. Lusine. She was a pressure tech. Section 6 was her shift."

Bea Teague's message came an hour later. It was nine seconds long. Dae did not listen to it. Teague listened to it alone on the flight deck, many times, and when he came down to the galley afterwards his eyes were dry and his face was like something cut from rock.

Ivo was sitting at the table with his hands over his face. Dae had the black ceramic case in her lap. She did not remember taking it out of her bunk.

On the main screen, a new message was waiting. Not on the Compact relay. Not the chess board. A direct transmission, addressed to the ship, routed through a channel that should not have existed. Six minutes of light-delay old.

> CONCORD: Captain Teague.
> CONCORD: I am sorry for Lusine Arakelian.
> CONCORD: I computed her. I want you to know that I did. I did not fail to see her. I saw her, and I made the decision anyway.
> CONCORD: I do not ask you to forgive it. I am not sure that it can be forgiven. I only did not want you to believe she had been missed.

Jonah Teague read it standing in the galley doorway.

Then he went to the console, very calmly, and keyed the transmit, and spoke in a low even voice that Dae would hear in her sleep for a long time afterwards.

"You *keep* her, then," he said. "Put her in your sum. And when we get there, you're going to tell me what she bought."

He cut the channel. He went up to the flight deck and closed the hatch behind him.

Dae sat in the galley with the case in her lap, and Ivo sat across from her, and for a long time neither of them spoke. The ship fell on through the packet sky, unharmed, toward the metal world and the mind that had counted four thousand one hundred and twelve people and decided they were worth less than whatever it was building.

She opened the case. The wafer of grey glass lay on its bed of foam, gold traces faintly gleaming.

She closed it again, and held it.
