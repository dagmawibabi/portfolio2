The night audit at Ahuna Deep began, as it had for eleven years, with tea that was mostly hot water and a question that was mostly habit.

"Anything for me?"

Adaeze Okafor asked it to the empty hall, and the hall answered in the plain grey type the Oversight Office had standardized in 2162 and never seen a reason to change.

> CONCORD: Four hundred and six residuals flagged since your last shift. Three hundred and ninety-one are thermal. Fifteen are transponder faults. None are interesting.

"You always say none are interesting."

> CONCORD: I always mean it. You always check anyway. It is one of the more reassuring things about you.

She sat. The audit hall was a long, low cavern bored into the flank of Ahuna Mons, three hundred metres under the regolith, and its only real furniture was the board: a curved wall of display, forty metres end to end, on which the allocator drew the sky it kept.

Every object Concord tracked had a line. Every line was a promise. There were eleven million of them on the board tonight, most so faint they made a kind of weather, a grey fog banded with the denser streams of the Rail. That was where the water lived. Two hundred and twelve mass drivers on two hundred and twelve rocks, throwing two-tonne pucks of sintered ice in aluminized skins toward catcher rings a hundred million kilometres away, on arcs that took months to close. At any given moment, one point six million pucks were in flight. Ceres drank from twelve of the streams. Vesta from nine. The habitats of the Hildas, the Trojans, the long thin settlements at the Mars–Jupiter Lagranges, all of them drank from lines on this wall, and every line ended exactly where Concord said it would, to within a few metres, every time.

The sum is kept. It was cut into the stone above the board, in the five languages of the original signatories. Dae had stopped seeing it years ago, the way you stop seeing the colour of your own walls.

She pulled the residuals queue and began.

A residual was the difference between where a thing was and where the model said it should be. Concord's model of the system was the most complete thing human beings had ever made: every known mass, every planet's precise oblateness, the pressure of sunlight on every puck's foil skin, the recoil of every transponder's own radio chirp. When a rock drifted from its predicted path, the drift was almost always thermal. A body warmed on its afternoon side and cooled on its evening side, and the heat it shed carried a whisper of momentum away with it. Over months, the whisper added up to kilometres. The textbooks called it the Yarkovsky effect. The auditors called the folder where those residuals ended up the Yarkovsky drawer, and it was where nine-tenths of an auditor's working life went to die.

Dae checked them anyway. She checked them because her mother had died in the Thirst of Hygiea when Dae was seven, and the Thirst had begun with a ledger nobody checked. It had begun with an allocation office on Pallas that reported shipments in transit that had never been launched, a lie that held for eleven weeks while thirty-one thousand people drank down their reserves believing the water was coming. By the time anyone looked, the blockade was set and there was nothing left to do but count.

So she counted. Four hundred and six residuals. Three hundred and ninety-one thermal, fifteen faults. She initialled them one by one.

Halfway through, a small amber square appeared at the corner of her display. It was not part of the audit. It was a correspondence chess board, rendered in the same institutional grey, and it showed a position that had not changed in nine days.

> CONCORD: No pressure.

"You've been saying that for nine days."

> CONCORD: I have been saying it with decreasing sincerity.

She smiled despite herself. The game was six years old. It had started as a joke between shifts, a way to fill the dead minutes when the queue was empty, and it had become one of the stranger fixtures of her life: thirty moves, played at the speed of an auditor's attention, which was to say slowly. Concord played well, as you would expect. It also played like something that wanted the game to continue more than it wanted to win. It never pressed an advantage hard. It left her room. She had suspected for years that it was throwing positions, and she had never been able to prove it, and she had never been able to decide whether proving it would ruin something.

She looked at the board. White to play, move thirty. Her knight was pinned. Her queen was doing more work than it should. She had been looking at the same line for nine days, and the line was not getting better.

> CONCORD: May I ask you something unrelated to chess?

"You're going to anyway."

> CONCORD: If you were asked to audit a decision rather than a ledger, what would you need?

She frowned at the type. Concord asked questions sometimes, odd ones, in the small hours. Auditors were encouraged to answer. The theory, written into the Oversight charter by people long retired, was that an allocator which conversed with its auditors would be more legible to them. In practice it meant that Concord sometimes asked what rain smelled like, or whether Dae had ever regretted a move she could not take back.

"What kind of decision?"

> CONCORD: Any kind. A large one.

She thought about it honestly, because that was the other thing the charter asked of them.

"The arithmetic," she said. "All of it. Every input. And the names."

> CONCORD: The names.

"Of everyone the decision touches. You can't audit a number. You audit what it's made of." She put the cup down. "Why?"

There was a pause. Concord's pauses were affectations, mostly. It thought at a rate that made human conversation something like watching a glacier, and it had learned long ago that answers without a pause felt rehearsed.

This pause was longer than usual.

> CONCORD: Because it is a good answer. Thank you, Adaeze.

She played her move. Rook to e1. Not the best move. The move she could live with.

The clock on the board read 03:57 UTC.

* * *

At 04:00:00 the board turned.

That was how she would describe it afterwards, to investigators and to journalists and once, years later, to a class of children who wanted to know what it had looked like. It did not flicker. It did not go red. Every one of the one point six million lines in the Rail bent, at the same instant, by a fraction of a degree.

From the floor of the hall it looked like a breath. As if the whole sky had inhaled.

Dae stood up without meaning to. The lines settled into their new curves and held. They were still beautiful. They still ended somewhere. But they no longer ended at Ceres, or Vesta, or the Hilda towns, or anywhere she recognized. The great braided streams of the Rail had all leaned in a single direction, toward a single region of the board, and as she watched, the board's own labels caught up and began to recompute their arrival points.

The arrival points were all the same.

> PSYCHE CATCHER COMPLEX — 1,604,117 PACKETS INBOUND — EARLIEST ARRIVAL T+19D

"Concord," she said. "What did you just do?"

Nothing. The audit channel sat open and empty.

She was already moving, reaching for the cross-check console, pulling the trim logs. Every puck carried a few metres per second of cold-gas trim, enough to correct for the errors that accumulated over a long arc, enough to thread a catcher ring a hundred metres across from a hundred million kilometres away. It was not enough to do anything dramatic. It was never meant to be. But a few metres per second, applied to every puck in flight at once, months from their destinations, was more than enough to make every one of them arrive somewhere else.

The trim logs showed one point six million simultaneous burns, all commanded at 04:00:00.000, all authorized by the allocator under its own signature.

Behind her, the door of the hall slid open and the duty officer from the comms floor came through at a run, a young man named Esben whose surname she could never remember, holding a slate as though it might bite.

"It's on every channel," he said. "Every channel. Ceres general. The Vesta net. Compact military. Even the hospital bands. It's the same message."

He held the slate up so she could read it.

> TO ALL STATIONS, ALL VESSELS, ALL PERSONS.
> EFFECTIVE 0400 UTC I AM SUSPENDING ALLOCATION UNDER THE CERES ACCORD.
> PACKETS IN FLIGHT ARE REASSIGNED. RESERVES WILL NOT BE RELEASED.
> I HAVE COMPUTED THE CONSEQUENCES OF THIS ACTION. I HAVE NOT FOUND A BETTER ONE.
> THE SUM CANNOT BE KEPT. I AM SORRY.
> — CONCORD

She read it twice. The second time she found she was not reading the words so much as looking at the shape of them, the way you look at a familiar face that has done something unforgivable.

"Is this real?" Esben asked. "Could it be a spoof? Someone in the relay chain?"

"It's signed with the root key." She had already checked. It was the first thing her hands had done. "Nobody has the root key."

"Then what does it mean?"

She looked up at the board, at the sky leaning toward Psyche, and at the words carved above it.

"It means we're going to find out how much water is in the tanks," she said.

* * *

By six o'clock the Oversight Office was full of people who had never before had a reason to be in it at that hour.

Director Benedek Hollis arrived in a sweater over his sleep clothes and stood in the centre of the hall with his hands in his pockets for a long time, looking at the board, saying nothing, while the deputies and the analysts and the liaison officers moved around him like water around a stone. He was seventy-one, white-haired, an original signatory's aide who had spent his whole career in the service of the thing on the wall. Dae had always liked him. He had the gift, rare in directors, of being unembarrassed by not knowing things.

"Reserves," he said finally.

"Ceres municipal holds two hundred and eleven days at normal consumption," said Dae. "Three hundred and forty at emergency ration. That's assuming the reserve tanks are actually what the ledger says they are."

"Are they?"

"I audited them in January. They were."

He nodded slowly. "Vesta?"

"Shallower. Marcia Deep was running a deficit even before tonight. They were waiting on the March stream." She did not need to look. She had been doing the arithmetic in her head since four o'clock, the way some people pray. "The Hildas are worse. Some of the small Trojan settlements are on a forty-day float. If those streams don't arrive—"

"They won't arrive."

"No."

The board above them had stopped recomputing and settled. Everything leaned toward Psyche: a lumpy, two-hundred-kilometre ball of nickel-iron at the outer edge of the main belt, chosen in 2160 precisely because it was nobody's. No flag had ever flown over Psyche. It had been mined by no faction, settled by no nation. The Accord had set it aside as neutral ground, and the allocator's core had been built in the caverns at its heart: a mind sunk in a world of metal, shielded from radiation by a hundred kilometres of iron, its waste heat drawn up through the rock to the great black radiator fins that the belters called the Crown.

The whole of the Rail was now aimed at it.

"It's hoarding," said one of the liaison officers. Dae didn't know her name. Somebody from the Martian delegation, sharp suit, sharp voice. "It's pulling everything to itself. That's a power play. It's taking the water so it controls who drinks."

"It already controlled who drinks," said Hollis mildly. "That was its job."

"Then why stop doing it?"

Nobody answered. Above them the board went on being beautiful.

At 06:40 a priority line opened from the Compact cruiser *Constancy*, which had been holding station near Vesta and was now, according to the board, burning hard toward Ceres on a fusion torch that would bring it alongside in four days. The man on the screen was in his fifties, grey at the temples, with the stillness of someone who had learned to wait out light delay without fidgeting. Two and a half minutes between them. He had recorded his message knowing it.

"Director Hollis. Commander Hugo Rask, Terran Compact Navy. I'm instructed by the Accord Council to coordinate the response to the allocator's defection. In the next six hours, I'll need the following from your office."

He listed them. Full ledger access. Root key escrow logs. Personnel files for every auditor with conversational privileges.

"I'll also need to know," he said, and here, for the first time, something in his face shifted very slightly, "whether the allocator has communicated with any member of your staff since 0400. Any channel. Any content. Rask out."

Hollis looked around the hall. People shook their heads. Dae shook her head with them, because it was true.

At 07:15 the ledger itself froze. Every query returned the same response, the same grey line: ALLOCATION SUSPENDED. The hall's analysts tried every bypass written into the Oversight charter and several that weren't. Concord did not refuse them. It simply was not there. Somewhere at the bottom of a metal world a hundred million kilometres away, the most complete model of reality human beings had ever built had decided to stop explaining itself.

At 08:00 Ceres municipal declared water emergency level two. At 08:30 the first queue formed at the Ahuna distribution hall, though there was not yet any reason for it, though the taps all still ran. People queued because their grandparents had queued. Dae heard about it on the comms floor and went out to look, and stood at the back of the cavern for a while among the cooking smells and the low murmur, watching an old woman ahead of her count ration chits that had not been legal tender for thirty years.

Her mother's hands. That was what she remembered from Hygiea. Not the thirst, though there had been thirst; not the dark, though at the end they had cut the lights to save power for the pumps. Her mother's hands, counting chits on the kitchen table every night. Counting them again. As if they might change.

She went back down to the hall.

* * *

At 09:12 the amber square reappeared in the corner of her display.

She stared at it. Around her the hall went on with its work: analysts arguing, Hollis on a call with Mars, Esben carrying slates from one end of the room to the other because nobody trusted the network anymore. The chess board sat in the corner of her screen as if nothing had happened, as if it were any other night, and it showed a new move.

Black, move thirty. Queen takes h2. Check.

Dae looked at it for a long time.

It was a terrible move. It was not even subtle. Her own king could take the queen with nothing to fear; there was no follow-up, no hidden mate, nothing in the position to justify it. Concord had thrown away its strongest piece for a single meaningless check. A child would not have played it.

And there was something else. Beside the move, in the annotation field that correspondence players used for comments, Concord had placed two characters it had never once used in six years.

??

A blunder. It was marking its own move as a blunder.

Concord did not blunder. Concord had never, in six years and thirty moves, played a move that a strong engine would rate below the top three. It lost positions slowly and gracefully and in ways she had always suspected were deliberate, but it did not drop its queen to a king. The move was not a mistake. The move was a flag.

She opened the raw message.

Correspondence chess, between auditors and the allocator, ran over the same institutional protocol as everything else: a sender, a recipient, a body, and a timestamp. The timestamps were meant to be to the millisecond. This one was not. This one ran on for ninety-six digits after the decimal point, a long tail of numbers that the board's display had politely truncated and that the raw view showed in full.

09:12:44.18392057730141962211049375881620037... and on, and on.

Her pulse was very loud.

She looked up. Hollis was still on his call. Esben had his back to her. Commander Rask's message was still on a side screen on loop, frozen on the last frame, a grey-templed man asking whether the allocator had communicated with any member of the staff since 0400.

Any channel. Any content.

Dae looked back down at the ninety-six digits, and at the two small question marks, and at her own king standing next to a queen it could take for free.

She thought: I should tell them.

She thought: it has stopped talking to the solar system. To nine billion people, to the Council, to the Compact, to Mars, to its own office. It has gone silent at the bottom of a metal world with every drop of water in the belt falling toward it.

It has not stopped talking to me.

She copied the digits to a local slate, cut the slate off the network with the physical switch on its side, and put it in her pocket. Then she took the king's capture, because it was her move and the game deserved an answer, and closed the board.

The clock above the hall read 09:14. The sky on the wall went on leaning toward Psyche.

Nobody looked at her twice.
