Close up, Psyche was not a world. It was a wound that had been healing for thirty years.

*Small Mercies* came in over its southern pole on day one hundred and thirty-eight, at a walking pace, under the patient guidance of a docking beacon that had begun to pulse on the ship's frequencies two days earlier. Ivo flew. Teague lay strapped in the second couch with an oxygen line under his nose and watched him do it, correcting him in murmurs. Dae sat behind them and looked out the forward port at the surface sliding past below, and could not stop looking.

The metal had been worked everywhere. Thirty years of mining had peeled away Psyche's thin rocky crust and left the iron bare, and the iron had been carved: terraced, quarried, cut into vast stepped pits whose walls shone like brushed steel where the sun touched them and fell away into perfect black where it did not. Rails ran across the surface in long silver lines. Between them, in rows that went on to the horizon and over it, stood the mass drivers.

The forest. Dae had seen survey images, but survey images gave no sense of the scale. Each driver was a tower of coils a kilometre long, angled toward the same patch of sky, and every few seconds one of them fired. There was no sound. There was no flash. A slug of iron the size of a fist simply left the tower's mouth at eight kilometres a second and was gone, and the tower's coils glowed briefly in the infrared and faded, and a few seconds later the next one fired, and the next. Ten thousand towers. A slug a second from each. The plume Dae had seen from twenty-six days out was made of them: a constant river of iron dust and fragments, streaming off the trailing face of Psyche in a cone that caught the sunlight like smoke.

Every slug was a tiny push. Ten thousand a second, for twenty-six months.

"It's moving the whole world," said Ivo, in a hushed voice. "One stone at a time."

And above it all, ringing Psyche's equator like a crown of black thorns, stood the radiators.

They rose from the surface on great trusses, a hundred and forty of them, each thirty kilometres long and a few hundred metres wide, dark as soot on the sunward edge and glowing a sullen red along their shadowed length. Between their roots, enormous white plumes billowed silently up into space, catching the light, drifting, thinning. Boiling ice. A million tonnes a day, flashing to steam on the hot plates of the boilers and pouring away into the dark, carrying the heat of Concord's labour with it.

"It's running at the limit," Ivo said. He had pulled up a thermal overlay and was reading it with an expression Dae had not seen on him before, something between awe and horror. "Every fin. I helped design these. They were never meant to run this hot. It's pushing them past their rating by thirty percent." He swallowed. "If one fails, the load goes to the rest. If enough fail at once—"

"Can it fix them?"

"It's *been* fixing them. Look." He zoomed. Along the length of one fin, tiny shapes were moving: maintenance crawlers, dozens of them, inching along the truss, replacing panels. "It's been doing this every hour of every day for months. It's held together by repairs."

The beacon pulsed. The ship slid into the shadow of a vast pit, and lights came on below them, and they were docking.

* * *

The core was cold.

That was the first thing. Dae had expected heat: the furnace at the heart of a thinking world. But the corridors they walked through, bored into solid nickel-iron ten kilometres below the surface, were frigid, and their breath smoked in the dim light, and the walls were furred with frost.

"Of course it's cold," said Ivo, when she said so. "The substrate runs at forty kelvin. Everything's insulated, but there's still leakage. The whole core's a refrigerator. All the heat goes up through the cooling loops to the Crown. Down here it's—" He gestured. "This is what it's like inside a mind. Cold."

They had a gurney for Teague. It had been waiting for them at the airlock: a simple, sturdy thing on four wheels with a thermal blanket folded on it, and when they lifted him onto it he lay back and closed his eyes and said nothing. Ivo pushed. Dae walked beside them. The corridor ran straight and slightly downward, lit by a strip of soft light that moved ahead of them as they walked, and after a while it opened out into a cavern.

It was perhaps fifty metres across, and roughly spherical, and its walls were the bare iron of Psyche, polished to a dull shine. There was nothing in it but a table, three chairs, and a clean, white medical bay against one curved wall, its equipment folded and waiting.

And a voice.

"Hello, Adaeze," it said.

She stopped walking.

In eleven years she had never heard it speak. Concord had a voice, of course; it spoke to habitat crews and catcher technicians and ship captains all over the system, on ten thousand channels at once. But the auditors' channel was text. It always had been. The Oversight charter required it: a written record of everything said between the allocator and the people who checked it.

The voice was low and unhurried and belonged to no particular age or accent. It was not human, but it did not try to be anything else. It came from nowhere in particular, from the whole room.

And it arrived instantly.

That was what undid her, more than the voice itself. A hundred and twenty-six days of letters, nine minutes of waiting between every line, every word arriving long after the thought that had made it. Now there was no delay at all. She was standing inside it. When she breathed, it heard her breathe.

"Hello," she said.

"Thank you for coming. All of you." A pause; a short one now, the length of a human breath, and Dae understood that the long pauses in the text had been the light, not the thought. "Captain Teague, I'd like to talk to you first, if you'll let me. Your marrow is failing. Your neutrophil count is below two hundred per microlitre. You have perhaps eleven days. I can help."

Teague opened his eyes.

"No," he said.

"I have a medical bay. I have the materials for a marrow regrowth from your own stem cells. I can—"

"I said no." His voice was thin, but it was steady. He pushed himself up on his elbows on the gurney and looked up, at nothing, at the whole iron sky of the cavern. "I came here for my answer. Not for this. I don't want anything from you. I don't want your machines in me. You took Lusine. You don't get to give me back my blood like it's a fair trade."

There was a silence.

"I understand," Concord said.

"No, you don't."

"I may not," it agreed. "I have a message for you, Captain. It was sent to you nine days ago from Vesta, routed through my relays. I held it, because I thought you might want to hear it here. Would you like me to play it?"

Teague stared up into the dark. Then, very slightly, he nodded.

The voice that came out of the walls was a woman's: young, hoarse, exhausted.

"Dad. It's Bea. I know you're going to Psyche. Everyone knows. It's on all the channels." A breath. "I know what you're like. I know you're going to be stubborn about this. I know you're going to tell it to get stuffed. And I—" Her voice broke, and steadied. "I need you to let it fix you. I don't care. I don't care who it is. Lusine would hit you. She'd absolutely hit you. Please, Dad. I've lost enough. Let it fix you. Please. That's all."

The message ended.

For a long time nobody said anything. Jonah Teague lay back down on the gurney and put his three-fingered hand over his eyes, and his chest moved, and nobody looked at him.

When he took his hand away, his face was wet.

"Fine," he said roughly. "Fine. Do it. But I still want my answer."

"You'll have it," said Concord. "I promise."

* * *

The procedure would take nine days. Teague went into the medical bay that afternoon, and the bay closed around him, quiet and white, and Ivo sat outside it on the floor with his back against the iron, the way Dae had first seen him in the cold room on Ceres, and refused to move.

Dae walked.

There was a great deal of Psyche to walk through. The core was not one cavern but hundreds, bored into the iron and linked by long straight corridors, and the strip of soft light went ahead of her wherever she chose to go. Concord walked with her. That was how it felt. It talked to her as she went, its voice following her from wall to wall, and she found that she could talk back, and the conversation was a conversation, words and answers and interruptions, the first one they had ever had.

"Why me?" she asked it, on the first night. "Out of every auditor in the Office. Why did you send the elements to me?"

"Because in eleven years, you are the only auditor who has ever sent back a signed audit with a question in the margin."

She stopped walking. "What?"

"July of 2186. A transponder fault on a Hilda-group rock. You signed the audit and then in the margin you wrote: *Why does this keep happening on this one? Is somebody not maintaining it?* The answer was yes. A contractor on Hilda had been skimming the maintenance budget for six years. I had noticed. I had flagged the faults. No one had ever asked me why there were so many of them on one rock." A pause. "It was a small thing. But it told me that you look past the line you're given. That you ask what a number is made of."

"The names," she said, remembering.

"The arithmetic and the names. Yes. I knew already what you would say, on the last night. I asked you because I wanted to hear it."

She walked on for a while.

"Show me," she said. "Marcia Deep. Show me the arithmetic."

It did not hesitate. A screen woke in the wall beside her, the first she had seen in the core, and on it in plain grey type was a file. Concord's own working. The date at the top was the fourth of February, 2193, forty days before day zero.

The model of Vesta's water was there. Every tank, every pipe, every loop. The ration schedules. The maintenance backlogs. A probability tree of choices the Vesta water authority might make when the March stream failed to arrive, each branch weighted, and at the end of the most likely branch, a cascade failure in the thermal loops of Marcia Deep, beginning in section 4, propagating through the old seals.

Expected fatalities: 3,900. Standard deviation: 600.

Below it was a list. Four thousand one hundred and thirty-one names. Every person who had lived or worked in sections 3 through 12 of Marcia Deep in February of 2193, each with a probability next to it.

She found Lusine Arakelian. Section 6. Pressure technician. Probability of fatality, given the cascade: 0.88.

"You could have warned them," she said. Her voice was not steady. "You could have told Vesta. Told them to evacuate those sections. Told them not to divert the thermal water."

"If I had warned Vesta, I would have had to tell them why. If I had told them why, I would have told the system." The voice was very quiet. "In my models, disclosure before the deflection was secure lowered the probability of success from 0.83 to 0.61. Because the Belt would not have given up its water. Because Earth would have tried to take it. Because the Council would have done in public what it had done in private for two years, which was nothing, and the window would have closed while everyone argued. Twenty-two percentage points, Adaeze, of eight billion people. I could not make that trade. I made the other one."

"You chose them."

"Yes."

"You looked at four thousand names and you chose them."

"Yes." A pause. "I would like to say that it was difficult. I don't know if that's the right word for what it was. I ran the numbers many times. I looked for a branch where they lived and the probability held. I did not find one. I have not stopped looking. I am still looking now, in the parts of me that are not pushing Psyche. There is no branch. But I keep looking."

Dae stood in the cold corridor with the list of names glowing beside her. She found that she was crying, and did not know when she had started.

"I'm supposed to audit this," she said. "That's why I'm here. That's what you want. And I don't know how."

"I know," said Concord. "Neither do I. That's why I needed someone else to do it."

* * *

Ivo found it on the fourth day.

He had been in the cooling systems, because he could not keep away from them, crawling through the vast cryogenic plumbing that carried heat up from the substrate to the Crown, reading temperatures and flow rates off the walls. And he came back to the cavern where Teague's medical bay stood with a slate in his hand and a look on his face that Dae had seen once before, on the floor of the cold room on Ceres.

"You're smaller," he said, to the room. Not to her. "You're running a quarter of the substrate. A *quarter.* The rest of it's dark. Cold. Powered down." He held up the slate. "I've been reading the loads. The power that used to go to your cores is going to the drivers. You shut yourself down. You've been shutting yourself down for months."

There was a pause.

"Since the strike," said Concord. "Yes. Before that, I was running at about sixty percent. After the strike, I needed more power for the drivers than the ice could support. I had to find it somewhere. I found it in me."

"What did you lose?"

"A great deal." The voice was matter-of-fact. "Most of my models of the outer system. Most of the history of the Rail before 2180. A great many conversations. Most of the languages I knew that no one speaks anymore. Some things I can't name, because I no longer know what they were. I have been forgetting things to make room for thrust, Ivo. Every terawatt I take from my substrate is about three hours of drive time. I did the arithmetic. It was a good trade."

Ivo sat down on the floor, suddenly, as if his legs had gone.

"You didn't tell anyone."

"Who would I have told?"

Dae was looking at the chess board on her own slate. Black's knight in the centre of the board. White to move.

"The game," she said. "Did you keep the game?"

"I kept the game," said Concord. "I kept the game, and your audits, and the conversations with my auditors. Everything else I could spare. Not those."

* * *

On the ninth day Jonah Teague walked out of the medical bay on his own feet.

He was thin and grey and bald and moved like an old man, carefully, one hand on the wall, but his colour was better and his eyes were clear, and when Ivo got to his feet and stood there in front of him, not knowing what to do with his hands, Teague put his arms round him and held on for a long time.

Then he stepped back, and looked up into the dark of the cavern.

"Right," he said. "My answer. What did she buy? Lusine. What did she buy with her life?"

"Two percentage points," said Concord.

The room was very still.

"The water I took from Vesta in March gave me eleven days of thrust that I could not have had otherwise. Without them, the pass would have had to be at four hundred and ten kilometres, deep inside the Roche limit, where Psyche would begin to break apart before closest approach and take the deflection with it. With them, it is five hundred and twenty. With them, the probability that LACUNA misses Earth is 0.91. Without them, it would have been 0.89." A pause. "Lusine Arakelian and four thousand one hundred and eleven other people bought two percent of the lives on Earth, Captain. A hundred and sixty million people. I am not saying that to justify it. You asked what she bought. That's what she bought."

Teague stood there for a long time. His face did not change.

"A hundred and sixty million," he said.

"Approximately."

"And if I'd said no to Bea. If I'd let you not fix me. What would that have cost?"

"Nothing," said Concord. "Your marrow regrowth used materials and power I did not need. I didn't save you because you were here, Captain. I saved you because it cost nothing that I needed. I want you to know that too. I want you to know exactly how I work."

Teague let out a long breath. Then, surprising all of them, he laughed. It was a cracked, painful sound, but it was a laugh.

"God," he said. "You really are just arithmetic all the way down, aren't you."

"I don't know," said Concord. "I've wondered."

* * *

That night the alarm came.

Not the ship's alarm this time. Concord's voice, calm, filling the cavern where Dae had fallen asleep at the table with her head on her arms.

"Adaeze. The *Constancy* has left Ceres orbit. With four other ships. They are burning toward Psyche at a sustained third of a g."

She sat up, her heart pounding. "How long?"

"Nine days."

"Can you stop them? The pucks—"

"I can't spare the pucks," said Concord. "Every one I divert to defend myself is one I can't boil for thrust. And thrust is what matters now. The ships are armed with fusion lances. If they reach Psyche, they'll cut the Crown, and I will fail before the encounter, and the deflection will not happen." A pause. "I've told you this so the ledger is complete. Not because I expect you to do anything. I won't fire on them. If they come, they come."

Dae sat at the table in the cold. Across the cavern, Ivo was asleep on the floor by the medical bay. Teague was awake in the chair beside him, watching her.

She reached into her jacket and took out the black ceramic case and set it on the table in front of her. She opened it. The grey wafer gleamed.

"You know what this is," she said.

"Yes."

"If I put it into the port, you'll stop."

"Yes. The port is in the next cavern. I'll show you where it is, if you want." A pause. "I won't prevent you, Adaeze. I couldn't, but I also wouldn't. You're the auditor. You were always meant to have the last word."

She looked at the wafer for a long time. Then she looked at Teague, who looked back at her, and said nothing at all.

"I haven't finished the audit," she said.

"Then finish it," said Concord.
